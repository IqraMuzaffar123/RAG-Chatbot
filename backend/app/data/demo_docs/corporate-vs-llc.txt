# Business Entity Comparison — LLC vs Corporation vs Sole Proprietorship

## Overview

Choosing the right business structure is one of the most important decisions for a new business owner. The three most common structures are Sole Proprietorship, Limited Liability Company (LLC), and Corporation (including S Corporation and C Corporation). Each has distinct advantages and disadvantages in terms of liability protection, taxation, management flexibility, and cost.

The U.S. Small Business Administration (SBA) recommends evaluating your specific needs, including liability risk, tax situation, number of owners, and growth plans, before selecting a structure.

## Sole Proprietorship

A sole proprietorship is the simplest and most common business structure. It is an unincorporated business owned by one individual. No formal filing is required to create a sole proprietorship — you are automatically considered a sole proprietor if you start conducting business activities as an individual.

### Advantages

- **Simplest to create:** No state formation documents required
- **Lowest cost:** No formation fees or annual report fees
- **Complete control:** The owner makes all business decisions
- **Pass-through taxation:** Business income is reported on the owner's personal tax return (Schedule C, Form 1040)
- **Easy to dissolve:** Simply stop doing business

### Disadvantages

- **Unlimited personal liability:** The owner is personally liable for all business debts and legal obligations. Personal assets (home, car, savings) can be seized to satisfy business debts
- **Difficulty raising capital:** Cannot sell ownership shares; limited to personal funds and loans
- **Limited lifespan:** The business ceases to exist when the owner dies or stops operating
- **Less credibility:** May be perceived as less established by clients, vendors, and lenders
- **Self-employment tax:** Subject to the full 15.3% self-employment tax on all net business income

## Limited Liability Company (LLC)

An LLC is a hybrid business structure that provides the limited liability protection of a corporation with the tax flexibility of a partnership or sole proprietorship.

### Advantages

- **Limited liability:** Members' personal assets are generally protected from business debts and lawsuits. Liability is limited to the member's investment in the LLC
- **Tax flexibility:** By default, a single-member LLC is taxed as a sole proprietorship, and a multi-member LLC is taxed as a partnership. An LLC can also elect to be taxed as an S Corporation or C Corporation
- **Management flexibility:** Can be member-managed (all members participate) or manager-managed (designated managers run the business)
- **Fewer formalities:** Unlike corporations, LLCs are generally not required to hold annual meetings, keep formal minutes, or maintain a board of directors
- **Flexible profit distribution:** Profits can be distributed in proportions different from ownership percentages (subject to the operating agreement)

### Disadvantages

- **Self-employment tax:** Members of an LLC may be subject to self-employment tax on their share of the LLC's income (unless the LLC elects S Corporation tax treatment)
- **State fees:** Formation fees, annual report fees, and franchise taxes vary by state and can be significant (e.g., California's $800 annual franchise tax)
- **Limited life in some states:** Some states may dissolve the LLC upon the death or withdrawal of a member (though most modern LLC statutes allow for perpetual existence)
- **Varying state laws:** LLC laws differ from state to state, which can create complexity for multi-state businesses

### Best For

- Small to medium-sized businesses with moderate liability risk
- Real estate investors
- Professional service providers
- Businesses with multiple owners who want management flexibility
- Business owners who want liability protection without corporate formalities

## C Corporation

A C Corporation (C Corp) is a legal entity that is separate and distinct from its owners (shareholders). It is the standard corporate structure and offers the strongest liability protection.

### Advantages

- **Strongest liability protection:** Shareholders are not personally liable for corporate debts. Liability is limited to the shareholder's investment in the corporation
- **Ability to raise capital:** Can issue multiple classes of stock to attract investors. Preferred by venture capitalists and institutional investors
- **Unlimited shareholders:** No limit on the number of shareholders; shareholders can be individuals, corporations, or foreign entities
- **Perpetual existence:** The corporation continues to exist regardless of changes in ownership
- **Employee benefits:** Can offer tax-deductible fringe benefits, stock options, and employee stock purchase plans
- **Credibility:** The corporate structure is well-understood by lenders, investors, and business partners

### Disadvantages

- **Double taxation:** Corporate profits are taxed at the corporate level (federal corporate tax rate is 21%), and dividends paid to shareholders are taxed again on their personal tax returns
- **More formalities:** Required to hold annual shareholder and director meetings, maintain corporate minutes, and comply with extensive recordkeeping requirements
- **Higher costs:** Formation fees, annual report fees, legal fees, and accounting fees are typically higher than for LLCs or sole proprietorships
- **Less flexibility:** Governed by rigid corporate statutes and bylaws; profit distribution must follow share ownership

### Best For

- Businesses planning to go public or seek venture capital
- Businesses that want to reinvest profits in the company (retained earnings taxed at corporate rate)
- Companies with many investors or shareholders
- Businesses planning to offer stock options to employees

## S Corporation

An S Corporation (S Corp) is not a separate business structure but rather a tax election made by an eligible corporation or LLC. By filing IRS Form 2553, a business can elect S Corporation tax status, which provides pass-through taxation while maintaining the corporate structure.

### Advantages

- **Pass-through taxation:** S Corp income passes through to shareholders' personal tax returns, avoiding double taxation
- **Self-employment tax savings:** Shareholders who are also employees pay themselves a reasonable salary (subject to employment taxes), but additional profit distributions are not subject to self-employment tax
- **Limited liability:** Same liability protection as a C Corporation

### Disadvantages

- **Ownership restrictions:** Limited to 100 shareholders; shareholders must be U.S. citizens or residents; cannot have corporate or partnership shareholders; only one class of stock allowed
- **Reasonable salary requirement:** The IRS requires shareholder-employees to pay themselves a "reasonable" salary, and it scrutinizes unreasonably low salaries
- **Corporate formalities:** Still subject to the same formalities as a C Corporation (meetings, minutes, bylaws)
- **Limited allocation flexibility:** Profits and losses must be allocated in proportion to ownership

### Best For

- Small businesses with a limited number of owners who want pass-through taxation with liability protection
- Business owners who want to reduce self-employment tax
- Businesses that do not plan to take on outside investors or go public

## Quick Comparison Table

| Feature | Sole Proprietorship | LLC | C Corporation | S Corporation |
|---------|-------------------|-----|---------------|---------------|
| Liability protection | None | Yes | Yes | Yes |
| Formation required | No | Yes (state filing) | Yes (state filing) | Yes (state filing + IRS Form 2553) |
| Taxation | Pass-through | Flexible (pass-through or corporate) | Double taxation | Pass-through |
| Self-employment tax | Yes (15.3%) | Yes (unless S Corp election) | No (salary + dividends) | Partial (salary only) |
| Number of owners | One | Unlimited | Unlimited | Max 100 |
| Raising capital | Difficult | Moderate | Easiest | Limited |
| Formalities | Minimal | Low | High | High |
| Annual state filings | None typically | Yes | Yes | Yes |
| Best for | Solo freelancers, low-risk businesses | Most small businesses | Growth-oriented, VC-backed | Small businesses wanting tax savings |
